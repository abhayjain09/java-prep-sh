# Module 13 — Interview Questions

Organized by topic group, then by level (beginner → intermediate → senior → scenario), matching `java-basics/INTERVIEW.md`'s format. **System design is widely considered the single most decisive round in senior/staff loops at Amazon, Google, and Microsoft** — unlike a coding round, which is largely pass/fail on correctness, a system-design round is evaluated almost entirely on judgment: what you ask before designing, how you justify a trade-off, and how you respond when pushed on a weak spot. The same bar applies at S&P Global/JPMorgan/Goldman Sachs loops, usually with an added expectation of compliance/audit-trail awareness (Event Sourcing, HA/DR) given the domain.

## Group 1: Scalability & CAP/PACELC

**Beginner:** "What's the difference between vertical and horizontal scaling, and why can't you just always horizontally scale?"
*Ideal answer:* Vertical = bigger machine; horizontal = more machines behind a load balancer. Horizontal scaling only works correctly if instances are stateless — see this module's concrete example: `java-basics`' `Inventory` holding stock in an in-process `HashMap` means two horizontally-scaled instances would each have their own diverging view of stock and could oversell. State has to move to shared storage (a database or distributed cache) before horizontal scaling is safe, not just possible.
*Follow-up:* "What's a symptom in production logs/metrics that would tell you a 'horizontally scaled' service actually still has hidden in-memory state?" → Inconsistent answers depending on which instance served the request (e.g. a customer sees different stock counts on page refresh), or a bug that only reproduces when a specific request happens to land on a specific instance twice in a row (sticky-session-dependent behavior).

**Intermediate:** "Explain CAP theorem, and why is 'partition tolerance' not really an optional third choice?"
*Ideal answer:* CAP says you can't have Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance all at once during a partition. In practice P isn't optional because real networks *will* partition (packet loss, timeouts, cross-region latency) — so the actual engineering choice is CP vs. AP, not "pick any two of three."
*Follow-up:* "Give a data type from an order system that should be CP, and one that should be AP, and justify each." → Stock count during checkout: CP, because overselling is a real business incident more costly than a moment of unavailability. Product description: AP, because a few seconds of staleness costs nothing and blocking page renders on a consistency check would be a poor trade for that data.

**Senior:** "PACELC extends CAP with an 'else' branch for the non-partitioned case. Why does that matter more in practice than the partition case itself?"
*Ideal answer:* Partitions are relatively rare events; the latency-vs-consistency trade-off in PACELC's "else" branch is paid on *every single request*, all the time. A system that only reasons about CAP's partition scenario and ignores PACELC's steady-state latency cost of strong consistency will systematically under-estimate the day-to-day performance cost of a CP choice — e.g. requiring a quorum write or reading from a primary/leader for every stock check adds real, felt latency continuously, not just during rare partition events.
*Follow-up:* "How would you measure whether your CP choice's steady-state latency cost is actually acceptable?" → Track p50/p95/p99 latency specifically on the consistency-sensitive read/write path (e.g. the inventory-reservation call) separately from the rest of the system, and set an explicit latency SLO for it — if that SLO is being violated under normal (non-partitioned) load, the PACELC cost of the CP choice is too high for this system and needs revisiting (e.g. via the hot-key mitigations discussed in `mock-interview.md`).

**Scenario:** "Your team ships an inventory service that reads from a replica for speed. Customer complaints start: 'the site said in stock, but my order failed at checkout.' Diagnose and fix."
*Ideal answer:* This is a PACELC 'else'-branch latency-vs-consistency choice that was made silently rather than deliberately for a CP-sensitive data type — the availability page read hit a lagging replica showing stale (too-high) stock, then the actual reservation at checkout correctly enforced the real, lower count and failed. Fix: don't read the number displayed on a product page from the same consistency tier as the number the checkout reservation enforces — either accept and clearly UX-communicate that the displayed count is approximate ("only a few left!" rather than an exact number) if serving from a replica/cache, or route the specific reservation-decision read to the strongly-consistent primary as the README recommends for exactly this data type.

## Group 2: CQRS & Event Sourcing

**Beginner:** "What problem does CQRS solve that a single model doesn't?"
*Ideal answer:* A single model optimized for transactional write integrity (normalized, constraint-heavy) is often a poor fit for read patterns that want denormalized, pre-aggregated, fast-to-query data (dashboards, order history). CQRS splits the write model (`OrderCommandService`) from the read model (`OrderQueryService`), letting each be optimized independently instead of compromising both.
*Follow-up:* "Does CQRS require two separate databases?" → Not strictly — you could apply CQRS with one database but two different query/update code paths — but the *full* benefit (a read schema shaped completely differently than the write schema, or a different storage technology entirely, e.g. a search index for reads) usually does mean two physical stores.

**Intermediate:** "What's the practical cost of CQRS that a team adopting it needs to plan for?"
*Ideal answer:* The read model becomes eventually consistent with the write model — a real projection-lag window where "I just placed an order" might not immediately appear in the read-model-backed history view. This needs a read-your-own-writes mitigation (route that user's next few reads to the write-side database, or optimistically render client-side) or the "why isn't my order showing up" support tickets will follow. Also: two schemas to migrate/maintain, and a new operational component (the projection pipeline) that can itself lag or fail.
*Follow-up:* "How would you monitor projection lag in production?" → Track the time delta between an event being published on the write side and the corresponding read-model row being updated — alert if that delta exceeds an SLO, since a growing lag is often the first symptom of the projector falling behind or failing silently.

**Senior:** "How do Event Sourcing and CQRS relate — are they the same thing?"
*Ideal answer:* No — they're complementary but independent. CQRS is about splitting read/write models; Event Sourcing is about storing an entity as a sequence of events instead of current state. You can do CQRS without Event Sourcing (a normal command updates a normalized table, and a separate process projects that into a read model). You can do Event Sourcing without CQRS (replay events to reconstruct current state, serve reads from that same reconstructed state). They're frequently paired because Event Sourcing's query complexity (Section 5 — "what's the current state" requires replay or a snapshot) is naturally solved by maintaining a CQRS read-model projection that's kept up to date as events arrive, rather than replaying on every read.
*Follow-up:* "What's the operational cost of Event Sourcing that's easy to underestimate?" → Event schema evolution — events are immutable and kept forever, so a mistake in an early event's shape lives in the log permanently; you write versioning/upcasting logic in the projector rather than "fixing" historical events, and that logic accumulates over the system's lifetime.

**Scenario:** "A customer disputes that their order was ever cancelled — they insist they never clicked cancel. How does an event-sourced order system help resolve this, and how would a state-only system handle the same dispute?"
*Ideal answer:* An event-sourced system has an immutable, timestamped, ordered log of every event, including exactly what triggered `OrderCancelled` (a specific actor/system, a specific timestamp, and typically a reason/source field on the event itself) — the dispute is resolved by showing the actual event record, which is inherently tamper-evident audit evidence. A state-only system only shows the current `CANCELLED` status with no built-in record of who/why/when unless a separate audit-log table happened to be maintained alongside it — and if that audit table was ever out of sync with the actual state mutation (a very plausible bug, since it's not the same structural guarantee Event Sourcing provides), the dispute may be unresolvable with confidence.

## Group 3: Saga Pattern, Circuit Breaker, Retries, Rate Limiting

**Beginner:** "What is the Saga pattern, in one sentence, and what problem does it solve?"
*Ideal answer:* A sequence of local transactions across separate services, each with a defined compensating action, used to achieve an "all or nothing" outcome for an operation that spans multiple services with no shared database transaction available.
*Follow-up:* "How is this different from what a database transaction already gives you?" → A database transaction's rollback is automatic and guaranteed by the database engine within one database; a saga's "rollback" (compensation) is manually defined application logic that must be explicitly written per step, is not instantaneous, and — unlike a true rollback — leaves a real, observable trace that something was attempted and undone (the compensating action actually ran, e.g. a refund was issued) rather than appearing as if nothing happened at all.

**Intermediate:** "Walk through `java-basics`' `OrderService.placeOrder()` and explain, line by line, how you'd generalize it into a saga."
*Ideal answer:* (Candidate should walk the direct mapping this module's README Section 6 and `lld/saga-orchestrator.md` lay out: the `Deque<OrderLine> reserved` local variable becomes a `CompensationRegistry`; the `try`/`catch(InsufficientStockException)` becomes `SagaOrchestrator.run()`'s per-step try/catch; `inventory.reserve()`/`inventory.release()` become a `ReserveInventoryStep`'s `execute()`/`compensate()` calling a remote `InventoryServiceClient`; `throw e` preserving the cause becomes `SagaStepException` wrapping the original failure.) The key added complexity: each step is now a network call that can fail in new ways (timeout, lost response), the registry must be durable enough to survive an orchestrator crash, and compensations must be idempotent because of retry semantics that a single in-process method call never had to worry about.
*Follow-up:* "Why must compensations be idempotent specifically, when the original Module 1 `release()` call didn't need to worry about that?" → Because a distributed compensation call can be retried (by the orchestrator recovering from a crash, or by a network-level retry) without certainty about whether the first attempt actually succeeded — a non-idempotent `release`/`refund` would double-release stock or double-refund a customer if invoked twice for the same reservation/payment.

**Senior:** "Compare orchestration and choreography for a saga, and justify a choice for the order-placement flow specifically."
*Ideal answer:* (See README Section 6's full table.) Orchestration centralizes the flow in one component — easier to reason about and debug, but that orchestrator becomes a new coordination point that must itself be highly available with durable state. Choreography distributes reactions across services via events — lower direct coupling, easier to add new reactive consumers, but harder to see or debug the whole flow from one place. For order placement specifically: a mostly-linear, well-defined sequence with clear compensations favors orchestration for its debuggability; a domain with many independent parallel reactions to one event (e.g. fan-out notifications) would favor choreography.
*Follow-up:* "What would make you switch this specific flow to choreography later?" → If the number of independently-owned teams each needing their own autonomous reaction to order events grows large enough that the orchestrator becomes a bottleneck for every team's changes (everyone needs to modify the shared orchestrator to add their service's participation) — at that point, choreography's lower coupling starts to outweigh orchestration's easier debuggability, especially if strong distributed tracing is already in place to compensate for choreography's harder-to-follow flow.

**Scenario:** "Your circuit breaker in front of the Payment service keeps flapping (opening and closing rapidly) during a traffic spike, even though Payment isn't actually down — just briefly slow. Diagnose and fix."
*Ideal answer:* This is very likely a threshold-tuning problem: either the failure-rate threshold is too sensitive to brief latency blips (treating a temporarily-slow-but-working dependency as "failed"), or there's no separate slow-call threshold distinguishing "erroring" from "successful but slow," so normal load-driven latency variance is being misclassified as failure. Fix: widen the rolling window used to calculate failure rate (so a few slow calls don't dominate the ratio), tune the slow-call-duration threshold to reflect a latency the system can actually tolerate rather than an arbitrary default, and separately verify whether the *retries* upstream of the circuit breaker (without jitter) might themselves be creating the load spike that's making Payment look slow in the first place — a classic case where fixing the retry strategy (add jitter, cap attempts) resolves what looks like a circuit-breaker tuning problem.

## Group 4: Microservices, Modular Monolith, DDD, Clean Architecture

**Beginner:** "What's a modular monolith, and how is it different from 'just a monolith'?"
*Ideal answer:* A modular monolith is one deployable application, but internally organized into modules with enforced boundaries (each module exposes a narrow public interface; internals aren't reachable directly from other modules) that mirror real business capabilities/bounded contexts. "Just a monolith" (a "big ball of mud") has no such internal boundaries — everything can reach everything, making a future split, or even just safely changing one area without breaking another, much harder.
*Follow-up:* "How do you enforce a module boundary in Java specifically?" → Package-private classes for internals with only application-service interfaces public, the Java Platform Module System (`module-info.java`), or physically separate Maven/Gradle modules within one deployable artifact — increasing levels of enforcement rigor.

**Intermediate:** "A colleague says 'we should build this as microservices from day one so it's easier to scale later.' How do you respond?"
*Ideal answer:* Push back with the concrete cost microservices impose immediately: network calls replacing method calls, the full Saga-pattern machinery replacing a database transaction, N deployment pipelines and on-call rotations instead of one, and a much higher cost of a wrong bounded-context boundary (a distributed-systems migration instead of a package refactor). Recommend a modular monolith with enforced internal boundaries instead — it gets the *design* benefit (clear seams, ready to extract later) without paying the *operational* cost until a genuine scaling or team-ownership need actually justifies a specific extraction.
*Follow-up:* "What's the risk of the modular-monolith approach, and how do you mitigate it?" → The risk is boundary discipline eroding over time without the physical enforcement a real service split would provide (a tempting shortcut: reaching directly into another module's internals "just this once"). Mitigate with build-level enforcement (module system, or even a static-analysis/architecture-test rule like ArchUnit checking that module internals aren't imported cross-module) rather than relying on convention alone.

**Senior:** "Define a bounded context and explain how it should map to the eventual microservices split, if one happens."
*Ideal answer:* A bounded context is a boundary within which one model and its ubiquitous language apply consistently — e.g. this domain's Ordering, Inventory, Payments, Shipping, and Customer/Identity contexts (see the context map). The claim: bounded contexts should be the *same* lines a future microservices split cuts along, because they represent genuine independent areas of the domain with their own language and change-rate — splitting along technical layers instead (a "database service," a "business logic service") produces a distributed monolith with network cost but no independent-deployability benefit, since a single business change would still require deploying multiple layer-services together.
*Follow-up:* "What DDD relationship type would you use between your system and a third-party payment gateway, and why not a Shared Kernel?" → An Anti-Corruption Layer, not a Shared Kernel — a Shared Kernel implies both sides agree to change a shared model together, which isn't realistic with an external vendor whose API you don't control. An ACL translates the vendor's model into your domain's own vocabulary at the boundary, so a vendor API change or migration to a different provider doesn't ripple into your core domain.

**Scenario:** "Six months after launching your modular monolith, the Payments module's team wants to extract it into its own service, citing PCI compliance needs. Walk through how the modular-monolith design specifically makes this easier than it would otherwise be, and what still has to change."
*Ideal answer:* Because the Payments module already exposed a narrow application-service interface (not direct access to its internals/tables) and sat behind an Anti-Corruption Layer to the actual payment gateway, the extraction is largely "stand this module up as its own deployable behind the same interface, and turn what were in-process calls into network calls" — the domain logic inside Payments doesn't need to be rediscovered or rewritten. What still has to change: the calling code (e.g. `ChargePaymentStep`) now needs the resilience patterns (circuit breaker, retry-with-jitter) that an in-process call never needed; the two systems no longer share a database transaction, so anything that used to be atomic across Payments-and-something-else now needs saga-style compensation if it wasn't already saga-based; and the team needs new observability (distributed tracing) to debug across the new service boundary that a single process's logs used to make trivial.

## Group 5: Observability, HA/DR

**Beginner:** "Name the three pillars of observability and what each is best for."
*Ideal answer:* Metrics (aggregatable numeric time-series — dashboards, alerting thresholds, trend-spotting), logs (detailed discrete records of individual events — "what exactly happened here"), traces (a single request/operation's journey across services — the causal chain and timing of every hop).
*Follow-up:* "Which of the three is most specifically needed once you adopt the Saga pattern, and why?" → Distributed tracing — a saga inherently spans multiple services, and without a propagated trace ID correlating them, reconstructing "what happened to this specific order" means manually correlating timestamps across separate log streams, which doesn't scale past a couple of services.

**Intermediate:** "What's RPO, what's RTO, and how are they different?"
*Ideal answer:* RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time — how far back your backup/replication can safely lag. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is how long you can afford to be down before service is restored. They're independent: you can have a tight RPO (near-zero data loss via synchronous replication) with a loose RTO (a slow, manual failover process), or vice versa — designing for one doesn't automatically satisfy the other.
*Follow-up:* "Why is synchronous cross-region replication usually rejected for an interactive checkout flow, even though it would give the tightest possible RPO?" → Every write would have to wait for acknowledgment from a data center potentially hundreds of miles away before the customer's request completes — that added latency, paid on every single checkout, all the time, is usually a worse trade than accepting a small async-replication RPO window (a few minutes) for the rare case of a full regional failure.

**Senior:** "Justify a specific RPO/RTO target for an order-management system at a mid-size company, and explain what achieving a materially tighter target would cost."
*Ideal answer:* (See README Section 13 — candidate should propose concrete numbers, e.g. ~1-5 minute RPO / ~15-30 minute RTO for cross-region DR, near-zero for multi-AZ, and justify each against the cost of achieving tighter numbers.) A materially tighter RTO (near-zero, via active-active multi-region) requires resolving the same CAP/PACELC consistency-vs-latency trade-off across regions on every write, all the time — the cost isn't just infrastructure spend, it's a continuous latency tax on the entire system's normal operation, which should be an explicit, costed business decision, not a default "always minimize RTO" engineering instinct.
*Follow-up:* "How would you actually verify your stated RTO is achievable, rather than just asserted?" → Scheduled failover drills/game days that actually exercise the failover runbook (not just tabletop review) — an untested DR plan is not a real capability, and the drill itself often surfaces gaps (stale runbooks, missing permissions, unexpected dependencies) that pure documentation review wouldn't catch.

**Scenario:** "During a real regional outage, your team discovers the DR region's data is 40 minutes stale — well outside your stated 5-minute RPO. What likely went wrong, and how do you prevent recurrence?"
*Ideal answer:* Likely causes: replication lag wasn't actively monitored/alerted (the 5-minute RPO was a design assumption, not a continuously-verified fact), a backlog built up silently before the outage (e.g. from an earlier, unnoticed network degradation or a write-volume spike the replication pipeline couldn't keep up with), or the async replication mechanism itself had a partial failure that wasn't caught by health checks. Prevention: instrument and alert on actual replication lag as a first-class metric with an SLO tied directly to the stated RPO (so a lag approaching the RPO threshold pages someone *before* a disaster makes it matter), and include replication-lag-under-load as a specific scenario in regular DR drills — not just testing failover mechanics, but testing whether the RPO promise actually holds under realistic pre-failure conditions like a write-volume spike.
